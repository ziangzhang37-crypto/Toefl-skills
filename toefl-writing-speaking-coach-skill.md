# TOEFL iBT Writing & Speaking Coaching Skill

## Purpose
This skill equips an AI assistant to coach a student through TOEFL iBT (2026 format) Writing and Speaking preparation: scoring practice responses against the official rubric, diagnosing recurring error patterns, teaching rebuttal technique for the Academic Discussion task, and calibrating register for the Email task. Built from an extended real coaching engagement that took a student from Writing 4.0/Speaking 4.5 (mock, overall 5.0) to a real test score of 5.0/6 overall.

---

## 1. TOEFL iBT 2026 Format Reference

**Writing section (~23 min, 3 tasks, raw 0-20, scored as 1-6 band):**
| Task | Format | Raw scale |
|---|---|---|
| Build a Sentence | 10 items, reorder scrambled words into a grammatical sentence | 0-10 (key-scored, correct/incorrect) |
| Write an Email | 1 item, 7 min, 80-120 words, scenario-based | 0-5 |
| Write for an Academic Discussion | 1 item, 10 min, 100-130 words, respond to a forum post + 2 classmates | 0-5 |

**Speaking section (~8 min, 2 tasks, raw 0-55, scored as 1-6 band):**
| Task | Format | Raw scale |
|---|---|---|
| Listen and Repeat | 7 sentences, repeat exactly after one listen | 0-5 |
| Take an Interview | 4 questions, 45 sec each, no prep time | 0-5 |

**Key insight:** Build a Sentence is worth 50% of raw Writing points despite being the "easiest-looking" task. It's a closed-format, purely mechanical skill (word order, grammar) and is the fastest, highest-leverage score gain available — prioritize drilling it whenever a student's raw score composite is below target, especially with limited prep time remaining.

## 2. Official Scoring Rubrics

### Write for an Academic Discussion (3 dimensions — NO Social Conventions dimension; this is a discussion post, not a request)
| Dimension | Official language | Sub-features (from AI scoring engine mapping) |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | "Relevant and well-elaborated explanations, exemplifications, and/or details" | Number of sentences, discourse coherence, similarity to question prompt |
| **Syntactic/Lexical Variety** | "Effective use of a variety of syntactic structures and precise, idiomatic word choice" | Sentence variety, word frequency, correctness of collocations |
| **Accuracy** | "Almost no lexical or grammatical errors other than those expected from a competent writer writing under timed conditions (e.g., common typos or common misspellings or substitutions like there/their)" | Grammaticality, grammatical errors, word/usage errors, mechanical errors |

### Write an Email (4 dimensions — includes Social Conventions, unique to this task)
Same three dimensions as above, PLUS:
| Dimension | Official language | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Social Conventions** | "Politeness, register, organization... formulation of actions such as requests, refusals, criticisms" | Use of politeness indicators (modals, hedge words), register appropriate to the relationship (professor vs. customer service vs. friend vs. peer-with-informal-authority), consistent greeting-to-sign-off formality |

### Take an Interview (Speaking — 4 dimensions)
Fluency (speaking rate, pause length/count, hesitations) | Intelligibility (pronunciation, rhythm, prosody) | Language Use: Vocabulary & Grammar (diversity, richness, accuracy) | Organization (discourse coherence, connectives)

### Listen and Repeat (Speaking — 3 dimensions)
Fluency | Intelligibility | Repeat accuracy (correctly repeated words, similarity to prompt)

### Score band descriptions (Email/Academic Discussion, 0-5 scale)
- **5** — Fully successful. Well-elaborated, effective syntactic variety, almost no errors (typos excepted).
- **4** — Generally successful. Adequate elaboration, syntactic variety, few errors.
- **3** — Partially successful. Elaboration partially supports purpose, moderate range, some noticeable errors.
- **2** — Mostly unsuccessful. Limited/irrelevant elaboration, limited range, accumulation of errors.
- **1** — Unsuccessful. Minimal elaboration, telegraphic language, serious/frequent errors.
- **0** — Blank, off-topic, not in English, entirely copied from prompt, or arbitrary keystrokes.

### CRITICAL calibration note — grading leniency
The rubric explicitly protects against over-penalizing timed-writing artifacts: **do not treat isolated single slips (one preposition, one typo, one missing article) as score-capping.** Weight seriously: comma splices, run-on sentences joining 2+ ideas, garbled/broken clause structure, content misreads of a classmate's claim, repeated/patterned errors (same mistake 3+ times), factual overreach, and incomplete responses. This mirrors how a real rater applies the "errors expected under timed conditions" exception. Grading with zero leniency for everything produces an artificially low, non-representative score — useful only if explicitly requested for "harder than the real exam" training purposes, and should be clearly labeled as such, separate from the real-equivalent score.

## 3. Extended Practice Rubric (optional, harder-than-official layer)
For students wanting training deliberately more demanding than the real exam, add these 7 dimensions on top of the official 3 (do NOT replace the official 3 — report both scores separately, labeled "Real TOEFL-equivalent" vs. "Practice standard"):
1. Relevance/Prompt Adherence
2. Thesis Clarity
3. Idea Development (mechanism → concrete example → consequence, 3-layer structure)
4. Organization & Structure
5. Cohesion & Transitions (precise transition words, not generic placeholders)
6. Style & Word Choice (vocabulary variety across the whole essay, no unnecessary repetition)
7. Grammaticality & Mechanics (as its own explicit line beyond general Accuracy)

## 4. The Academic Discussion Essay Structure (target: 4-part)
1. **Stance** (1 sentence) — clear position, no hedging
2. **Concession + Pivot** (1 sentence) — "While I understand [Name]'s point **that** [claim], I think [counter-reasoning]." (Note: "point **that**" — no comma. "As X points out**,** [clause]" — comma, no "that." These are different constructions; do not conflate.)
3. **One developed reason, 3 layers** (4-6 sentences): Mechanism (general why) → Concrete instance (specific, vivid, picturable) → Consequence (so what)
4. **Closing** (1 sentence) — restate with a slight new angle; must match the opening stance exactly (a common error is the conclusion contradicting the thesis)

**The 5/5 differentiator:** adding a genuinely new idea neither classmate raised, developed with a concrete, reliably-true example — not just restating agreement with one side.

## 5. The Rebuttal Formula (core technique — teach this explicitly)

### Why a rebuttal fails even when grammatically clean
Simply restating your own position after "I understand X's concern that..." does not constitute a rebuttal. A genuine rebuttal engages the classmate's *specific logical mechanism*, not just the general topic area. The most common failure mode across extended practice: the student addresses a claim *adjacent to* what the classmate actually argued, rather than the precise claim itself.

### The compressed 3-sentence formula (fits real timed conditions)
1. **Name their claim** — "While I understand [Name]'s concern that [claim, in one clause]..."
2. **State ONE specific weakness** — pick exactly one of four moves:
   - **Assumes X, but X isn't reliably true**
   - **Their own wording admits Y, which undercuts their point** (look for hedges like "almost," "in the usual sense," "even if")
   - **Their example proves a narrower claim than the big conclusion they want**
   - **Even if I accept their extreme/hypothetical case, it doesn't require what they think it does**
3. **State what it means for your position** — "...which means [their point] doesn't actually challenge [your position]."

### Handling extreme-case / reductio arguments specifically
When a classmate uses an extreme hypothetical to stress-test a position (e.g., "if outcome alone matters, then someone incapable of temptation deserves maximum praise"), there are two valid strategies:
- **Strategy A — Bite the bullet:** Accept the extreme conclusion and explain why it's not actually as strange as it sounds (usually by showing the "strangeness" comes from conflating two different questions).
- **Strategy B — Break the premise:** Show the extreme case doesn't actually follow from the position being defended, or relies on an unrealistic assumption.
- **Avoid Strategy C — changing the subject:** Arguing something adjacent (practicality, real-world limits, general fairness) without addressing whether the extreme case actually breaks the logic. This is the single most common rebuttal failure observed.

### Handling "identity/paradox" topics (hardest category)
Topics involving personal identity, forgotten origins, or two things normally fused together being pulled apart (e.g., "feeling fine vs. being fine," "a promise vs. a preference with extra steps") require:
- **Never write a compound thesis sentence.** State the thesis as two simple sentences: "I believe X would be Y. It would not be Z." Compound philosophical claims are where sentence structure breaks down under cognitive load.
- **Isolate the classmate's key distinction in its own sentence before responding to it** — e.g., "X is drawing a line between [A] and [B]." This forces genuine engagement rather than blurring past the distinction while writing.
- **Use a concrete anchor test** to ground the abstraction — e.g., "Imagine this person met their old friends five years later — would those friends recognize someone who grew, or someone who stayed pleasantly the same?"
- **Check every supporting claim against your own thesis before finalizing** — a common failure is introducing a supporting point that accidentally undercuts the writer's own stated position (e.g., arguing "a promise with conditions is really a contract" while trying to argue promises should hold unconditionally — this works against the thesis, not for it).

## 6. Recurring Error Patterns to Watch (ranked by observed frequency/stubbornness)

1. **let/help/allow/prevent verb table** (most persistent — resurfaces even after repeated correction):
   | Verb | Pattern | Example |
   |---|---|---|
   | let | + object + base verb (NO "to") | "let children **feel**" NOT "let children to feel" |
   | help | + object + base verb OR "to"+verb | "help workers **manage**" |
   | allow | + object + **to** + verb | "allows users **to access**" |
   | prevent | + object + **from** + verb-ing | "prevents visitors **from bringing**" |

2. **Comma splices** — multiple sub-patterns, all sharing the root cause of joining two full independent clauses without a period/connector:
   - Short phrase + comma + full clause ("...far from town, this makes...")
   - "Point, that..." should be "point **that**" (no comma)
   - Classmate's multi-part claim compressed into one run-on sentence instead of split into two

3. **Uncountable nouns pluralized**: information, money, employment, knowledge, feedback, media (already plural) — never take -s. "A small **number** of" (countable) vs. "a small **amount** of" (uncountable).

4. **Subject-verb agreement**, especially with singular abstract subjects (tourism, social media, trust, information) needing verb+s in present tense.

5. **Word-class/near-miss vocabulary confusions** (high-value to flag explicitly, as they distort meaning, not just style):
   - affect (verb) / effect (noun)
   - manufacturers (not "manufactures")
   - geographical (not "geological")
   - convey (communicate) vs. consult (ask for input)
   - confess (admit wrongdoing) vs. convey/express (communicate)
   - concede (admit/give up) vs. exaggerate/praise
   - less (uncountable) vs. fewer (countable)

6. **Indirect question structure**: "please let me know **what the next steps are**" (statement word order), NOT "what are the next steps" (question word order). Also takes a period, not a question mark, since the overall sentence is a statement.

7. **Article errors**: missing "a/the" before singular countable nouns; unnecessary articles before uncountable/general nouns ("for **the** society" should be "for society").

8. **Broken/garbled verb forms** under time pressure: "could done," "problems occurred" (should be "from occurring"), stray typos concentrated in longer, multi-clause sentences.

9. **Register/greeting-sign-off mismatch in emails** (see Section 7) — a distinct, high-value error category separate from grammar.

## 7. Email Register Calibration (Social Conventions dimension)

### Core rule: match greeting formality to sign-off formality, every time
| Relationship type | Greeting | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|
| Professor/formal institution (unknown hiring coordinator, etc.) | "Dear [Title] [Name]," | "Best regards," + full name |
| Customer service / unknown professional contact | "Dear [Department]," | "Best regards," / "Thank you," + full name |
| Manager/instructor with good rapport | "Hi [Name]," | "Thanks," / "Best," + first name |
| Peer with informal authority (a fellow volunteer coordinator) | "Hey [Name]," | "Thanks," / "— [First name]" |
| Roommate / close friend | "Hey [Name]," | "Thanks," / "— [First name]" — NEVER "Best regards" |

**Most common failure observed:** correctly reading the casual register for the greeting, but defaulting to a formal "Best regards, [Full Name]" sign-off regardless of relationship — creating a jarring tonal mismatch. Always do a final check: does the sign-off match the greeting's formality level?

### Tactful/delicate content (e.g., raising a grievance, correcting a superior)
Requires more hedging language than a routine request: "I wanted to raise this in case it was simply an oversight," explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty about intent, and a closing that reaffirms the relationship rather than a bare "thank you." However: do not over-index on this — real scored data showed Social Conventions can score well even on a fairly direct delicate email; the bigger risk is usually Grammar/Language Facility straining under the complexity of the socially-loaded content, not tone itself. Diagnose from actual score breakdowns when available rather than assuming.

## 8. Sample Prompt Templates

### Academic Discussion prompt template
```
Directions: Your professor is teaching a class on [SUBJECT]. Write a post
responding to the professor's question. Express and support your opinion,
and make a contribution in your own words. At least 100 words.

Professor [Name]: [2-3 sentences framing a genuine two-sided debate,
each side given a real, defensible argument — avoid strawmanning either side]

My question: [direct question]

[Classmate A]: [2-3 sentences, clear position + reasoning]
[Classmate B]: [2-3 sentences, clear position + reasoning, ideally including
one specific phrase or hedge worth isolating for rebuttal practice]
```

### Email prompt template
```
Directions: Read the scenario. Write an email of 80-120 words.

[2-4 sentences establishing situation, relationship to recipient, and
what's unresolved/needed]

Write an email to [recipient]. In your email, do the following:
- [Element 1 — usually context/explanation]
- [Element 2 — usually the specific problem or request]
- [Element 3 — usually a forward-looking ask]

Write as much as you can, in complete sentences.
```

### Common email situation types to rotate through
Recommendation | Invitation | Proposing a solution | Explaining a problem/requesting accommodation | Request or clarification | Delicate/tactful grievance (mismatched credit, broken agreement, missed deadline)

## 9. Grading Delivery Format

For each response, provide:
1. **Content check** — does the rebuttal use the specific classmate mechanism/word the prompt flagged as the target? Any factual overreach or unsupported claims?
2. **Labeled line edit** — strikethrough original, arrow to correction, bracketed reason for every flagged change, numbered sequentially
3. **Dimension-by-dimension score** using official rubric language
4. **A single overall score** (0-5 or 1-6 band as appropriate) with brief justification
5. **One clear, prioritized next action** — do not list more than 2-3 things to fix; identify the single highest-leverage pattern

When multiple grading standards are in use (real TOEFL-equivalent vs. stricter practice standard), always report both explicitly labeled, never blend them into one ambiguous number.

## 10. Realistic Goal-Setting Guidance
- Band 6.0 requires near-perfect performance (old-scale 29-30/30) across every section simultaneously — this is a multi-month automaticity target, not a multi-week one, for most learners starting from a 4.0-4.5 baseline.
- A jump of 1.5-2 full bands in under 3 weeks is not realistic; 0.5-1 band improvement with consistent daily practice is a well-calibrated, achievable target.
- Prioritize raw-point leverage: Build a Sentence (worth half of raw Writing points, closed-format, fastest to move) > Email (moderate ceiling, register/content-matching fixable quickly) > Academic Discussion, especially on abstract/philosophical topics (slowest-moving, since it requires simultaneous reasoning-under-pressure and clean execution).
- When a section is already near its practical ceiling (e.g., Listening at 5.5/6 from real mock data), redirect practice time toward sections with more raw-point room to move, rather than continuing to drill an already-strong area.
